The Pick & Weiss Family

Chapter One

Origins in Bohemia

The Kingdom of Bohemia, 18th–19th centuries

The Pick family belonged to the old Jewish communities of the Czech lands. They appear in the historical record as early as the 1793 Bohemian census, where a Pick household — a wool trader, his wife and children — is recorded in the Jewish quarter (Židovské Město) of Prague.1 Whether by direct line or by kinship, the name marks a family long rooted in Bohemia, generations before the events at the heart of this history.

By the later nineteenth century the family had settled in the spa region of western Bohemia, in the small town of Lichtenstadt — today Hroznětín — a short distance from the famous spa city of Karlsbad (Karlovy Vary). It is there that the story comes into sharp focus.

Chapter Two

The Pick Line

Lichtenstadt & Karlsbad, Bohemia · 1880s–1930s

At house number 34 in Lichtenstadt lived Sebald Pick, a merchant (Kaufmann), and his wife Flora, née Wohl, who came from Lobositz (Lovosice) in the Leitmeritz district. Theirs was a family touched early by loss: a daughter, Fanni, died in 1880 at the age of six, and her burial was recorded in the Jewish register of the community.2

On 22 June 1886, in the same house at Lichtenstadt no. 34, Sebald and Flora had a son: Ezechiel Pick.3 He would grow up to be a merchant like his father, doing business in Hroznětín.

A name, and the authorities who kept it

Ezechiel's birth record carries an unusual and telling detail. At some point the second given name "Mathias" was added, in brackets, beside his name. In 1906 the Imperial-Royal Governor's Office in Prague ruled that this addition had been unauthorized.3 But the matter did not end there. On 14 April 1931, the District Authority (Bezirkshauptmannschaft) in Karlsbad — together with the Jewish registry authority — formally recorded that the man registered as Ezechiel Pick bears the given name Mathias, reconciling his civil and religious identity at last.3

"The person registered here as Ezechiel Pick … bears the given name Mathias." — Marginal note in the birth register, dated 14 April 1931, signed by the district rabbi

Beyond the personal detail, these entries tell us something important: across more than four decades — 1886, 1906, 1931 — the civil and religious authorities of Austria-Hungary and then of Czechoslovakia kept and maintained this family's records. Ezechiel Mathias Pick was a man known to the state, embedded in the civil life of the Bohemian lands. It is why he appears in later documents under both names, Ezechiel and Mathias — one person, one identity, twice recorded.

Chapter Three

The Weiss Line

Freudenthal, Austrian Silesia & Vienna · 1878–1913

While the Picks were merchants of the Bohemian spa country, the other side of the family came from the railways and the imperial capital. Adolf Weiss, born in Krems on the Danube in 1878, was a railway official — in time a station master of the Imperial-Royal State Railways — stationed at Freudenthal (Bruntál) in Austrian Silesia.4

In Vienna, in 1902, he married Rosalie Prinz, born in the Ottakring district of the city in 1879, a daughter of Nathan Prinz and Regina Schnitzer.5 The couple raised their family in Freudenthal, where the railway ran. Their children included Leopold (1905), Gustav (1909), and a daughter born on 3 February 1913 — Else (Elsa) Weiss.6

It is Else who joins the two families together — and it is her brother Leopold who, decades later, would become the keeper of their memory.

Chapter Four

A Marriage of Two Worlds

Karlsbad · 1930s

Ezechiel Mathias Pick, the merchant of Hroznětín, married Else Weiss, the station master's daughter from Silesia. On 6 February 1937, in Karlsbad (Karlovy Vary), their son was born: Liebermann Pick.7

The birth was entered in the civil register, naming both parents — Ezechiel Pick, born 1886 in Hroznětín, and Else née Weiss, born 1913 in Bruntál.7 A family of three, in a prosperous spa town, on the eve of catastrophe. The child was little more than a year old when the world the family had known began to close around them.

Chapter Five

Persecution & Flight

Sudetenland → Slovakia → Hungary · 1938–1944

In the autumn of 1938 the Sudetenland — the German-speaking borderlands that included Karlsbad — was annexed by Nazi Germany. For the region's Jews, dispossession followed quickly. On 14 September 1940, the official Nazi gazette, the Deutscher Reichsanzeiger, published lists of Jews of the Karlsbad district whose property and rights were confiscated for the German Reich. Among the names: "Ezechiel Pick, born 22 June 1886 in Lichtenstadt."8

Stripped of home and livelihood, the family fled. Their path led out of Bohemia southward — to Lučenec in Slovakia, where, according to family testimony recorded after the war, Mathias Pick lived under the protection of the local rabbi.9 From there the road led onward to Budapest. It was a flight shared by countless families of the region, scattering ahead of the deportations — and it is why the family's traces appear afterward across three countries: born in Bohemia, sheltered in Slovakia, deported from Hungary.

Chapter Six

Deportation

Budapest & Kistarcsa, Hungary · 1944

In 1944, after the German occupation of Hungary, the machinery of deportation reached the family. A Hungarian transfer list dated 20 May 1944 records the men moved to the Kistarcsa internment camp outside Budapest. Two names appear together on that list: Ezechiel Pick, born 1886 in Lichtenstadt, and his young son Liebermann Pick, born 1937 in Karlsbad — father and child, deported side by side.10

Else, the mother, did not reach the camps. According to her brother Leopold's later account, she was shot at the railway station in Budapest during a transport.9 Of the family of three that had lived in Karlsbad seven years before, only the child would survive.

Chapter Seven

A Child Alone

Displaced-persons camps, Germany → the Land of Israel · 1945–1947

The boy survived. Orphaned and alone, he emerged from the war in the displaced-persons system of occupied Germany — passing through camps and children's homes at Leipheim, Bayerisch-Gmain, Bergen-Belsen and Hamburg-Blankenese.11 In the intake records his parents' fate is marked simply as unknown; the surviving papers describe a Hungarian-speaking child, slight for his age — he stood 123 cm and weighed 25 kg — yet judged by the examining physician to be in good general health.12

His registration documents variously name him as Czechoslovak and as Hungarian — the twin traces of a Bohemian birth and a Hungarian flight.11

Blankenese

His last stop in Europe was Hamburg-Blankenese, on the banks of the Elbe. There, in a villa on the river that had been turned into a home for Jewish child survivors, the Youth Aliyah movement gathered orphans of the war and made them ready for a new life in the Land of Israel. It was at Blankenese that the boy was taken formally in hand by the Jewish Agency: registered, cared for, and — on 17 June 1947 — examined by a physician who judged him fit to emigrate.12 His time at the Blankenese children's home is recounted in greater detail in the book Cherries on the Elbe.14

Is the applicant, in his physical and mental condition, suited for the Aliyah? — Yes. — Jewish Agency medical certificate, Blankenese, 17 June 1947

On 20 July 1947, aboard the ship Providence, a nine-year-old listed among the unaccompanied children reached the shores of the Land of Israel.13 He was received at a children's home on Mount Carmel. The last of the Karlsbad family had come through.

A new name

In his new country the child began a new life, and with it he took a new name. Liebermann Pick — born in the spa town of Karlsbad, orphaned in the Shoah, carried across a continent — became Moshe Liberman.7 The surname Pick, borne by the Bohemian merchants of Lichtenstadt for generations, was folded into the Hebraized Liberman; and it is under that name that his own family would grow in Israel in the decades that followed.

Chapter Eight

Remembrance

Munich · 1960

Fifteen years after the war, in 1960, Leopold Weiss — Else's brother, an uncle who had survived — sat down in Munich to write to the International Tracing Service at Arolsen. He sought official word of his lost relatives: his brother-in-law Mathias Pick and his sister Else Pick, née Weiss.9

His letter, careful and precise, set down what he knew: where they were born, where they had fled, and how they were lost. It is thanks to that act of remembrance that the threads of this family — the Bohemian merchant, the Silesian station master's daughter, the orphaned child — can be drawn together today into a single story. This history is written in their memory.
